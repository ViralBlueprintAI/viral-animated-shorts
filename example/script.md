# The Dancing Plague (Strasbourg, 1518) - final script (72 s, 220 words)

Facts (checked): July 1518, Frau Troffea starts dancing in a Strasbourg street; within weeks dozens, later reportedly up
to 400 people dance; the council and physicians blame "hot blood" and prescribe more dancing (the grain market and the
horse market are cleared, a stage is built, musicians and dancers are paid); deaths are reported by some chroniclers;
famine and disease had hit the region; the St Vitus legend held that the saint could curse people to dance; the
council finally banned music and sent the dancers to the St Vitus shrine at Saverne, with red shoes.
Sources: Wikipedia "Dancing plague of 1518"; John Waller, "A Time to Dance, a Time to Die" (2008).

In 1518, a city tried to stop people dancing to death by hiring a band.
You see, in Strasbourg, Frau Troffea steps into the street and starts to dance.
No music, no reason. She just can't stop.
Her husband begs her to come inside. She dances for six days straight.
By the end of the week, 30 neighbors have joined her.
Within a month, by some accounts, 400 people are dancing in the streets, day and night, until their feet bleed.
So the city council calls in the doctors. Their verdict? Hot blood.
The only cure is to dance it out.
So they clear the grain market, build a stage and hire musicians and professional dancers to keep everyone going.
It works perfectly. The dancing gets worse.
Some, people said, dance themselves to death.
So what was making a whole city dance?
Well, Strasbourg was starving. Famine, disease, and one terrifying legend: anger Saint Vitus, and he curses you to dance.
When you're that scared and that exhausted, your mind can start to believe the curse and your body follows, the same way one yawn spreads across a room.
Finally, the council banned music and sent the dancers to Saint Vitus' shrine in red shoes.
And it worked. The curse ended at the very place they believed had caused it.
